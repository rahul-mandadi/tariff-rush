  | ================ Q&A PREP ================
  | The deck is scoped to data, limits and next steps on purpose. These are not on the slides, but we should be able to answer all six cold.
  |
  | 1. DOMAIN PROBLEM. A tariff is announced with a future start date, importers rush ahead of it, and imports fall afterwards. Someone exposed to the targeted country has to decide on announcement day whether to wait it out or re-source, and the data that would settle it arrives about five weeks after each month closes. Slides 2 and 3.
  |
  | 2. DATA SCIENCE PROBLEM. Three, in order: a classification problem (does a surge occur), a regression problem (how large), and a duration problem (how long does the aftermath last, with right-censoring for episodes still running at the end of the panel). Slide 4 states them in plain language; this is the technical framing.
  |
  | 3. WHO AND WHAT. Whoever carries exposure to one origin rather than to total volume: an importer deciding whether to requalify a supplier, a lender holding that as credit risk, a carrier running that origin's service. We are explicit that we are still testing which of those the data serves best. An earlier version of this project named port and terminal operators; we tested that and dropped it, because displaced volume arrives from a different country through substantially the same US customs districts.
  |
  | 4. DATA SOURCES. Two, both public and free. US Census international trade API for monthly imports, pulled at HS10 (slide 5). The Federal Register API for tariff actions, 2,792 documents classified down to 134 real actions (backup slide 9). The product lists behind each action come from the USITC Harmonized Tariff Schedule, chapter 99.
  |
  | 5. METHODOLOGY. Pull at HS10 because tariff orders are written at HS8 and HS10 nests inside it, so matching is exact rather than approximate. Build a counterfactual per series from pre-event data only. Then compare a tariffed product from a tariffed country against the same product from countries that were not tariffed, in the same month. That last step is the important one: a commodity price shock moves every origin together and cancels, while a tariff moves one origin against the rest.
  |
  | 6. EVALUATION AND METRICS. Every estimate is paired with a placebo: the same calculation with every event shifted back 24 months. If a result does not separate from its own placebo we do not report it as a result. That discipline has already retired three candidate methods. For the predictive questions, forward-chaining validation by year and grouped by case, so no case appears in both folds and the model is always tested on cases later than the ones it learned from, scored against a deliberately hard naive benchmark rather than against zero.

  | LAYMAN CONTEXT. Assume nobody knows what a tariff is.
  |
  | A tariff is a tax on imports. The government announces it, and it starts on a named date weeks or months later.
  |
  | That gap creates an obvious incentive: get your goods in before the tax starts. Importers rush, and you can see it in the copper line climbing before the date.
  |
  | Afterwards imports usually fall below normal, because buyers already stocked up.
  |
  | This behaviour is called front-loading. That is the whole subject of the project.

  --- NOTES ---
  | THE DOMAIN PROBLEM. This slide says why anyone should care.
  |
  | Somebody has to act on announcement day. An importer with a supplier in the tariffed country has to decide whether to wait it out or qualify a new supplier somewhere else, and requalifying runs months and costs real money. A lender behind either side holds the same question as credit risk. A carrier running that origin's service has to decide whether to keep it.
  |
  | And you cannot just wait and watch, because the data is late. By the time two bad months show up, the duty has been in force for a quarter.
  |
  | So the decision is made before the answer is knowable. That is the gap we are trying to close.

  --- NOTES ---
  | THE KEY SLIDE. If time runs short, this is the one that must survive.
  |
  | A tariff order does not say 'chapter 74, copper'. It points at a list of 8-digit product codes, usually published in a separate legal notice. Until you read those lists, the only thing you can do is treat a whole chapter as tariffed whenever an action touches anything inside it.
  |
  | We read them: 9,083 distinct 8-digit codes across 162 actions.
  |
  | The typical action covers about 5% of its chapter's value. A third cover under 1%.
  |
  | So chapter-level treatment means roughly 95% of what you are measuring is trade the tariff never touched. Any real reaction in the 5% gets averaged away by the 95% that did nothing.
  |
  | That is exactly what happened to us. Our first attempt to measure an effect found nothing, and this is why. The null was our ruler, not the world.
  |
  | The fix is to work at the 8-digit level, where a tariff either names a product or it does not. Everything after this is built that way.
  |
  | -- NARRATE IT THROUGH COPPER, which they already know from slides 2, 5 and 6 --
  | Ten separate tariff actions touch copper, chapter 74. Three of them cover more than half the chapter: 95%, 59%, 59%. The other seven cover under 1%, and several are civil-aircraft carve-outs that name a single product line. The median action touching copper covers 0.1% of it.
  | So if you mark copper as tariffed whenever any action fires, seven times out of ten you have flagged an entire chapter over something touching less than one percent of it. That is what the chart shows, across all 1,350 action-chapter pairs.
