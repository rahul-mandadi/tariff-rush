## 7. And this is where it nearly came apart

**RAUNAK · 1:42 · clock at end 7:52**

A tariff order never says "chapter 74, copper". It points at a list of specific eight digit
codes, usually in a separate legal notice. So if you haven't read those lists, all you can do is
mark a whole product group as tariffed whenever any action touches anything inside it.

That's what we did. And we got nothing.

So we went and read the lists. 9,083 eight digit codes.

Figure 5 is what we found. Each bar counts a tariff action paired with a product group. The typical
one covers about five percent of that group's value, and the red on the left is a third of them
covering less than one percent.

Copper again. Ten actions touch copper. Three cover more than half of it. The other seven cover
less than one percent.

And that's why we got nothing. If you label a product group tariffed when ninety-five percent of it
wasn't, any real reaction in the five percent gets averaged away by the ninety-five percent that
did nothing. The zero wasn't telling us about the world. It was telling us about our ruler.

It was one of three reasons, and it's the one that generalizes.

The fix is to work at eight digits, where a tariff either names a product or it doesn't. Everything
from here is built that way.

## If asked

**"So is there a rush?"**

We have the data and the product level matching to answer it now. The product group version of the question was unanswerable, for the reason on slide 7.

**"Who exactly is the customer?"**

Whoever is exposed to one country rather than to total volume: an importer requalifying a supplier, a lender behind either side, a carrier running that route. We are still testing which the data serves best. An earlier version of this project named port and terminal operators; we tested that and dropped it, because displaced volume arrives from a different country through much the same US customs districts.

**"Five percent of what, exactly?"**

The median over 1,350 action and product group pairs. Per action the median is 1.3 percent, so the slide is the conservative version.

**"What were the other two reasons?"**

Our announcement dates are mostly implementing notices, so the median official window is nine days and there is almost no anticipation period left in the data. That is what the copper story answers: the rush runs on news, not on documents. And the partner dimension was collapsed, which the across-origins design fixes.

**"Your copper chart recovers, so what is the problem?"**

Correct, and we are not claiming copper proves anything. In tonnage it goes 293 thousand tonnes in July 2025, down to 93 thousand in November, back to 245 thousand by August 2026. One product, one event. It is on slide 2 to explain front-loading.

**"Why not just watch the data and react?"**

That is slide 3. The first usable data lands about five weeks after the month closes, and the decision is on announcement day.

**"What is your data science problem, in technical terms?"**

Classification for whether a surge occurs, regression for how large, and duration analysis with right-censoring for how long the aftermath lasts.
