# Use-case evidence: do U.S. importers decide "switch supplier country or wait" after AD/CVD and Section 301 duties?

Compiled 2026-11-06 from public sources only. The Floor & Decor passage (Section B, first row) was re-checked in the filing text itself. Every claim has a URL and a date. Items marked UNVERIFIED were seen only in a search-engine summary or a secondary source and were not opened at the primary source.

How the ITC reports were read: usitc.gov refuses automated fetchers (HTTP 403), so the public PDFs were opened in a browser session and their text was extracted page by page. Page references below use the printed page label in the report (for example II-33, or 2.14 in the newer format) plus the PDF page index, so they can be checked by hand.

## Summary

- (a) Importers do make this decision. In every ITC final report checked, a large share of responding purchasers said they had changed suppliers during the period of investigation: 27 of 54 (aluminum extrusions), 23 of 45 (steel nails), 17 of 25 (PET resin), 11 of 22 (low-melt PSF), 13 of 37 (glass wine bottles), 8 of 24 (litho plates), and 4 of 24 (forged steel fittings). Most of that switching had other causes, such as supply shortages, COVID and bankruptcies. Only a handful of purchasers per case tie a switch to the trade case itself. Public filings (Floor & Decor, Hooker, Universal Electronics, GoPro, iRobot, Cooper Tire) show firms making and timing the decision.
- (b) When: the evidence points to action at or before Commerce's preliminary determination, not at the ITC final.
  - Floor & Decor stopped importing the covered Chinese tile between the preliminary and the final.
  - Mattress makers moved out of Vietnam, Thailand and Cambodia within weeks of the October 2020 preliminary duties.
  - Solar module deliveries were cancelled or delayed within a week of Commerce opening an inquiry.
  - The ITC itself attributes a March 2024 import spike in aluminum extrusions to the coming preliminary determination.
  - The one documented reason to wait is that provisional deposits are refunded when the ITC votes negative.
- (c) How long and how costly: purchaser-reported time to qualify a new supplier varies widely by product.
  - Ranges reported: 2 days to 1 year (PET), 1 to 180 days (nails), 14 to 180 days (litho plates), 5 to 365 days (wine bottles), 3 to 365 days (aluminum extrusions), and 1 to 18 months with an average just over 7 months (tin mill).
  - A trade-association submission to the USITC puts qualification of a new supplier for regulated products at 12 to 18 months.
  - Moving production for Section 301 took companies about 6 months to 2 years.
  - Named costs include qualification costs, supply refused by the old supplier, surcharges, and price increases of 5 to 15 percent.
- (d) Public trade data: the USITC, Kearney, Panjiva and trade press all use Census or DataWeb import statistics to track supplier-country shifts after duties. I found no public source showing an importer's sourcing team using DataWeb for this decision. That link is still unvalidated.

## Section A: ITC final staff reports, purchaser evidence

Scope note: the purchaser questions about "changed suppliers" cover the whole period of investigation, and they are not specific to the trade case. Respondents are a self-selected subset of the purchasers staff contacted. The counts come from questionnaires and are not a random sample.

### A1. Aluminum extrusions from 14 countries (final negative, Oct 30, 2024)

Report: Aluminum Extrusions from China, Colombia, Ecuador, India, Indonesia, Italy, Malaysia, Mexico, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey, United Arab Emirates, and Vietnam, Inv. Nos. 701-TA-695-698 and 731-TA-1643-1644 and 1646-1657 (Final), USITC Publication 5560, November 2024. https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub5560.pdf

- Supplier qualification (p. II-32, PDF p.198): 44 of 55 responding purchasers require suppliers to be certified or qualified. Reported time to qualify a new supplier ranged from 3 to 365 days. Six purchasers said a domestic or foreign supplier had failed to qualify or lost approved status since 2021.
- Changed suppliers (p. II-33, PDF p.199): half of reporting purchasers (27 of 54) changed suppliers since January 1, 2021. Reasons given were performance, capacity, price, service and diversification to reduce supply chain risk.
- Country shifts (pp. II-34 to II-35, Table II-14, PDF pp.200-201): purchasers reported mostly decreasing purchases of U.S. product. Some reported more purchases from China (not under order), Mexico and Turkey. Purchasers who raised Mexico purchases cited "sourcing alternatives", domestic supply limits and relocations from Asia. Purchasers who raised Turkey purchases described it as a deliberate supplier shift.
- Section 301 (p. II-4, PDF p.170): most producers (24 of 31), importers (74 of 96) and purchasers (44 of 56) said the 301 tariffs had no impact or did not know of one. Some importers said they responded to the 301 price increase by shifting sourcing to Vietnam and Thailand.
- Switching cost (p. II-16, PDF p.182): importers reported having to switch suppliers when domestic mills could not meet demand. They said this caused significant delays and qualification costs.
- Supply constraints pre and post petition (pp. II-14 to II-15, Table II-4, PDF pp.180-181): purchasers reporting constraints numbered 27 of 54 in 2021, 31 of 55 in 2022, and 9 of 55 in 2023 before the petition. After the petition (2023 to 2024) the count was 9 of 55 purchasers. Importers rose from 6 of 89 before the petition to 18 of 92 after it.
- Subject imports instead of U.S. product (pp. V-47 to V-48, PDF pp.365-366): 36 of 55 purchasers bought subject imports instead of U.S. product since 2021. Of those, 31 said subject prices were lower, and 11 said price was a primary reason. Non-price reasons included U.S. supply constraints, lead times and quality.
- Timing around the preliminary determination (Views of the Commission, PDF p.92, near footnote 448): the Commission said elevated subject imports in March 2024 would have been influenced by Commerce's upcoming preliminary determinations and possible provisional measures. This is direct agency evidence that importers time purchases around the preliminary.
- Refund if negative: customs broker Green Worldwide Shipping (Oct 31, 2024) told importers that provisional AD/CVD deposits collected during the investigation would be refunded after the negative vote. https://www.greenworldwide.com/usitc-finds-no-injury-from-imported-aluminum-extrusions-ends-investigation-without-new-duties/

### A2. Tin mill products from Canada, China, Germany (final negative, Feb 6, 2024; South Korea terminated as negligible)

Report: Tin Mill Products from Canada, China, Germany, and South Korea, Inv. Nos. 701-TA-685 and 731-TA-1599-1601 and 1603 (Final), USITC Publication 5492, February 2024. https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub5492.pdf

- Supplier qualification (p. II-47, PDF p.151): 18 of 26 purchasers require certification. Time to qualify a new supplier ranged from 1 month to 18 months, with an average of slightly more than 7 months. This is the longest average found in any report checked. 9 of 25 purchasers said at least one supplier failed to qualify or lost approved status since 2020. Two U.S. mills were among them. A Chinese producer failed a first trial in 2021 and qualified on a re-trial in 2022.
- Lead times (p. II-47): U.S. producers averaged a little over two months for produced-to-order product. Importers averaged a little over four months, with a range of six weeks to six months.
- New suppliers (p. II-22, PDF p.126): 8 of 26 purchasers said new suppliers entered the market since 2020. They named mills in India, Malaysia, Taiwan and Turkey, and mills in China, Serbia and Vietnam in general terms.
- Supply constraints (pp. II-22 to II-25, PDF pp.126-129): 18 of 26 purchasers had constraints before the January 18, 2023 petition. After the petition, 23 of 26 reported none. After the petition, one foreign producer reportedly stopped offering product to the U.S. market, and some importers stopped supplying or declined new orders because delivery schedules were uncertain.
- Purchase pattern changes (p. II-53, Table II-23, PDF p.157; repeated at p. V-22): 14 of 22 purchasers raised purchases from U.S. mills. Half of the purchasers buying from China and Germany raised those purchases. 8 of 13 cut purchases from nonsubject Taiwan.
- Section 301 (p. II-6, PDF p.110): 18 of 24 importers and 21 of 26 purchasers did not know whether the 7.5 percent tariff had any impact.

### A3. Steel nails from India, Oman, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Turkey (CVD final negative Oct 2022; AD final negative Jan 25, 2023)

Staff report: Steel Nails from India, Oman, Sri Lanka, and Turkey, Inv. Nos. 701-TA-673-675 and 677 (Final), USITC Publication 5370, October 2022. https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub5370.pdf . The staff report also carries data for Thailand. The AD final views are in Steel Nails from India, Thailand, and Turkey, Inv. Nos. 731-TA-1580, 1582, 1583 (Final), USITC Publication 5404, February 2023 (https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub5404.pdf). That 50-page publication contains views only and relies on a supplemental confidential report.

- Supplier qualification (p. II-21, PDF p.121): 18 of 44 purchasers require qualification. Time to qualify typically ranged from 1 to 180 days. Seven purchasers reported a supplier failing to qualify or losing approval since 2019, with failed suppliers named in South Africa, India, Oman, South Korea, the U.S., Turkey, Taiwan, Thailand and Mexico.
- Changed suppliers (p. II-22, PDF p.122): 23 of 45 purchasers changed suppliers since January 1, 2019.
- Reasons by country (p. II-23, Table II-9, PDF p.123): purchasers raised Thai purchases to diversify the supply chain. They raised Omani purchases partly because a plant in China closed and U.S. product was unavailable. Cuts were driven by quality, freight cost and availability.
- Search breadth (p. II-23): purchasers usually contact 2 to 5 suppliers. 14 of 44 changed how many suppliers they contact since 2019, and 9 of those 14 cited supply problems as the reason.
- Lead times (p. II-13, PDF p.113): one purchaser said import mills used to promise about 3 months from order to arrival. It said mills now quote 4 to 6 months and in practice take 8 to 12 months or longer.
- Unfulfilled demand (p. II-9, PDF p.109): purchasers obtained 98.2 percent of desired purchases in 2019. That fell to 89.5 percent in 2021 and 89.3 percent in Q1 2022.
- Section 301 on China (pp. V-5 to V-6, Table V-3, PDF pp.197-198): most importers and purchasers said the 25 percent 301 duty affected the market. Some purchasers said other countries now make products that China used to supply. That is a direct statement of country substitution after a tariff.
- Lost sales section (p. V-30, PDF p.222): staff contacted 122 purchasers and got 49 responses. In 2021 these purchasers sourced 9.0 percent from U.S. producers, 18.2 percent from subject countries and 62.8 percent from known nonsubject countries.

### A4. PET resin from Brazil, Indonesia, Korea, Pakistan, Taiwan (final negative, Oct 18, 2018)

Report: Polyethylene Terephthalate (PET) Resin from Brazil, Indonesia, Korea, Pakistan, and Taiwan, Inv. Nos. 731-TA-1387-1391 (Final), USITC Publication 4835 (dated November 2018 per the Federal Register notice). https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub4835.pdf

- Supplier qualification (p. II-14, PDF p.74): 23 of 25 purchasers require qualification. Time to qualify ranged from two days to a year, and 12 of 22 purchasers reported 60 days or less. Steps include trial runs on equipment, food-safety and lab tests, customer acceptance, audits and logistics. Three purchasers reported a supplier failing to qualify since 2015.
- Changed suppliers (pp. II-15 to II-16, PDF pp.75-76): 17 of 25 purchasers changed suppliers since January 1, 2015. Seven did so because of the M&G bankruptcy.
- Switching caused by the trade case (pp. II-15 to II-16):
  - Two purchasers said they reduced purchases of imports because of the antidumping investigations.
  - Purchasers reported cutting Brazilian purchases because antidumping duties were imposed.
  - One purchaser sharply increased purchases from Taiwan in 2018 despite the ongoing case, because of a shortage.
- New suppliers (p. II-16): 13 of 25 purchasers named new suppliers. Some were in nonsubject countries (Canada, Mexico, Russia, Vietnam, India, Turkey, Thailand, Oman, Malaysia, South Africa) and some in subject countries.
- Supply constraints (p. II-7, PDF p.67): 19 of 25 purchasers reported constraints. Some said earlier and current AD petitions and rulings had narrowed their options and raised import costs.
- Post-petition imports (pp. IV-10 to IV-11, PDF pp.108-109): for Indonesian and Korean firms with affirmative critical-circumstances findings, imports in the six months after the September 26, 2017 petition were lower than in the six months before. The exact percentage is redacted.
- Subject imports instead of U.S. product (pp. V-11 to V-12, Table V-13, PDF pp.133-134): 19 of 25 purchasers bought subject imports instead of U.S. product: 9 for Brazil, 3 Indonesia, 5 Korea, 7 Pakistan, 10 Taiwan. 11 said subject prices were lower. The text says four purchasers named price as a primary reason, while Table V-13 shows 5 for "any subject source". Eleven purchasers named availability or supply security as primary reasons.

### A5. Forged steel fittings from Taiwan (final affirmative, 2018; order issued)

Report: Forged Steel Fittings from Taiwan, Inv. No. 731-TA-1396 (Final), USITC Publication 4823 (released September 2018 per USITC listing). https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub4823.pdf . The staff report text covers China, Italy and Taiwan.

- Preliminary duties changed supply (p. II-6, PDF p.64): 9 of 35 importers reported supply constraints. Four importers said the preliminary duties had tightened supply from China, Italy and/or Taiwan. Among 7 of 23 purchasers with constraints, one said a subject source refused its order while the investigations were ongoing.
- Supplier qualification (p. II-13, PDF p.71): 7 of 24 purchasers require qualification. Time to qualify ranged from 10 to 180 days. Two purchasers reported a foreign supplier failing to qualify.
- Changed suppliers (p. II-13): 4 of 24 purchasers changed suppliers since 2015. The report states that firms changed suppliers because of these AD/CVD investigations, moving from subject sources to domestic or nonsubject sources. It also reports decreases in Taiwan purchases, including switches to domestic sources. Table II-8 shows 6 purchasers decreased Taiwan purchases and 1 increased them.
- New suppliers (pp. II-6 and II-13): 8 of 24 purchasers reported new entrants, including Indian and Korean manufacturers.
- Subject imports instead of U.S. product (p. V-8, PDF p.110): 19 of 25 purchasers bought subject imports instead of U.S. product. 17 said subject prices were lower, and 16 said price was a primary reason.

### A6. Low-melt polyester staple fiber from Korea and Taiwan (final affirmative, July 19, 2018; orders Aug 2018)

Report: Low Melt Polyester Staple Fiber from Korea and Taiwan, Inv. Nos. 731-TA-1378-1379 (Final), USITC Publication 4808, August 2018. https://www.usitc.gov/publications/701_731/pub4808.pdf

- Supplier qualification (p. II-12, PDF p.62): 15 purchasers require qualification. Seven reported 60 to 185 days to qualify and four reported 14 to 30 days. Methods were test runs, samples and lab tests.
- Changed suppliers (p. II-12): 11 purchasers changed suppliers and 11 did not. Suppliers added included the nonsubject Korean producer Huvis.
- Within-country nonsubject source (p. II-4, PDF p.54): most nonsubject imports came from Huvis, a Korean producer excluded from the case. Purchasers could therefore switch to a firm in the targeted country without changing country. Country-level import statistics cannot see this kind of switch.
- Availability changes (p. II-5, PDF p.55): 10 purchasers said the availability of Korean or Taiwan imports had changed, and some of them attributed this to the antidumping investigations.
- Subject imports instead of U.S. product (p. V-11, PDF p.99): 9 of 22 purchasers bought subject Korean product instead of U.S. product, 12 bought Taiwan product, and 15 bought nonsubject Korean product.

## Section C: practitioner, press, survey and research evidence

### C1. Official and practitioner guidance on timing

- U.S. Commerce Department (ITA), AD/CVD initiation FAQ (no date shown on page): CBP collects cash deposits only after Commerce's affirmative preliminary determinations. Entries before the preliminary are generally not liable unless critical circumstances are found, which allows retroactive duties for 90 days before the preliminary's Federal Register date. The ITC preliminary vote comes within 45 days of the petition. Commerce's CVD preliminary is due 65 days after initiation and the AD preliminary 140 days after, and both can be extended. https://www.trade.gov/faq/faqs-initiation-antidumping-duty-andor-countervailing-duty-investigation
- Harris Sliwoski LLP (Adams Lee), "How to Fight Back Against an AD or CVD Petition," Oct 22, 2020 (updated Sept 24, 2025): importers who can move to out-of-scope products or nonsubject-country suppliers may not need to take part in the case. The article warns that scope can be ambiguous, that CBP may require deposits until Commerce rules on scope, and that sourcing changes made mainly to avoid duties invite circumvention or evasion inquiries. It notes that most companies wait until after the petition to act. https://harris-sliwoski.com/chinalawblog/how-to-fight-back-against-an-ad-or-cvd-petition/
- Green Worldwide Shipping (customs broker), Oct 31, 2024: after the negative aluminum extrusions vote, provisional AD/CVD collected during the investigation is refunded to importers. This is the payoff to waiting when the final is negative. https://www.greenworldwide.com/usitc-finds-no-injury-from-imported-aluminum-extrusions-ends-investigation-without-new-duties/

### C2. Trade press on when importers moved

- Furniture Today (David Perry), Nov 23, 2020, citing a Piper Sandler report:
  - Mattress production that had moved from China to Vietnam, Thailand and Cambodia in early 2019 to avoid the China order was moving again, to Taiwan, Bangladesh and India.
  - The trigger was the preliminary AD rates announced in late October 2020, which were 191% to 990% for Vietnam, 573% to 763% for Thailand and 253% for Cambodia.
  - Manufacturers were moving equipment and employees. Piper Sandler expected import prices to rise 5 to 15 percent and import dynamics to normalize by mid-2021.
  - So the decision came within weeks of the preliminary, before the ITC final (April 2021).
  - https://www.furnituretoday.com/bedding-manufacturers/report-mattress-importers-moving-to-taiwan-bangladesh-and-india
- Home News Now (Thomas Russell), Aug 16, 2022, using import figures from a Mann Armistead & Epperson report:
  - Indonesia (AD rate about 2.2%) became the top U.S. mattress supplier in 2021 at $357 million, up from $169 million in 2019 and $6 million in 2018.
  - Vietnam fell from $348 million (2020) to $46 million (2021).
  - Mexico rose from $111 million to $283 million, and Taiwan from $31 million to $158 million.
  - https://homenewsnow.com/blog/2022/08/16/import-data-reveals-dramatic-shift-duties-have-had-on-global-mattress-sourcing/
- Solar Power World, Apr 5, 2022: about a week after Commerce opened circumvention inquiries on modules from four Southeast Asian countries (late March 2022), about 75% of 200 respondents to an SEIA survey said panel deliveries had been cancelled or delayed. https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2022/04/it-took-less-than-one-week-for-solar-panel-deliveries-to-the-united-states-to-be-canceled-or-delayed/ . UNVERIFIED: SEIA's later analysis of Apr 26, 2022 reportedly put the figure at 83% of module buyers. The PDF returned HTTP 403.

### C3. Surveys on share of firms that shifted after the 2018 tariffs

- AmCham China and AmCham Shanghai joint survey, fielded May 16 to 20, 2019, about 250 respondents, as reported by Al Jazeera on May 22, 2019:
  - Nearly 75% said the tariffs hurt their business.
  - More than 40% were considering moving, or had moved, manufacturing out of China, with Southeast Asia and Mexico preferred.
  - Fewer than 6% were considering the U.S.
  - https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2019/5/22/us-business-group-tariffs-are-hurting-us-firms-in-china . The AmCham press release itself (https://www.amchamchina.org/press/second-joint-survey-on-the-impact-of-tariffs/) is behind a bot-check page and was not read. UNVERIFIED secondary figures are 39.7% or 40.7%, with 24.7% to Southeast Asia and 10.5% to Mexico.
- Gartner survey, fielded Feb to Mar 2020, 260 supply chain leaders, reported by DC Velocity on June 24, 2020: 33% had moved sourcing and manufacturing out of China or planned to within two to three years. https://www.dcvelocity.com/articles/46383-gartner-survey-33-of-companies-are-moving-their-supply-chains-out-of-china
- Kearney 2024 Reshoring Index (2024):
  - Mainland China's share of U.S. manufactured imports from 14 Asian low-cost countries has declined steadily since the 2018 tariffs, reaching 46 percent by Q4 2023.
  - 38 percent of surveyed manufacturing executives were looking to continue moving operations out of China.
  - Sources cited are the U.S. Census Bureau and UN Comtrade.
  - https://www.kearney.com/documents/291362523/301101714/Made+in+America%E2%80%94Here+to+stay.pdf

### C4. USITC Section 232 and 301 economic impact report (2023)

Economic Impact of Section 232 and 301 Tariffs on U.S. Industries, Inv. No. 332-591, USITC Publication 5405, March 2023 (corrected May 2023). https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/pub5405.pdf

- p. 23 (Executive Summary): imports from China of covered products fell about 2 percent for each 1 percent of 301 tariff. The response grew over time, which the USITC says likely reflects importers adjusting and finding new sources.
- p. 101 and p. 125: contracts and purchase orders fix sourcing in the short term, so purchasers shifted to exempted countries (aluminum) or domestic production (steel) mainly in later years. For example, Canada supplied 69.7 percent of unwrought aluminum imports in 2021 after its exemption in May 2019.
- p. 151: apparel and retail associations told the Commission that sourcing shifts away from China are slow because alternative suppliers are often unavailable.
- p. 154: some computer equipment has strict prequalification requirements that make switching hard.
- p. 244 (National Foreign Trade Council submission):
  - A new supplier for regulated products must pass qualification testing and approval that can take 12 to 18 months.
  - Suppliers usually cannot be changed mid-contract without customer agreement.
  - UL-type safety approvals must be redone if the origin changes.
  - Some companies chose to pay the 301 tariffs rather than realign their supply chains.
- p. 251: seafood importers shifted sourcing away from China. Imports from all other sources rose from $19.0 billion (2018) to $25.8 billion (2021). I did not check whether this passage is staff text or a submission.

### C5. Academic research

- Flaaen, Hortacsu and Tintelnot, "The Production Relocation and Price Effects of US Trade Policy: The Case of Washing Machines," NBER Working Paper 25767 (April 2019), published in American Economic Review 110(7), 2020. Per the abstract, the 2012 AD duties on Korean washers moved production to China. The 2016 AD duties on China moved production to other export-platform countries, with only small price changes. This is the closest published evidence of country substitution after AD duties at the product level. https://www.nber.org/papers/w25767

## Section D: use of public trade data

- The ITC itself uses official import statistics, which come from Census and are served by DataWeb, for the country shares that decide cases.
  - Pub 5559 (p. 2.8) gives nonsubject import shares based on official statistics.
  - Pub 5560 (p. IV-19) bases its negligibility tests on the 12 months of import data before the petition.
  - Pub 5370 (p. V-6, fn 14) derives transport costs from official import data by HTS statistical number, accessed July 20, 2022.
  - Links are in Section A.
- USITC Pub 5405 builds its import tables from "USITC DataWeb/Census" (Table 4.3, p. 101). https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/pub5405.pdf
- Kearney's Reshoring Index is computed from U.S. Census Bureau and UN Comtrade trade data (2024 report, link in C3).
- Panjiva, S&P Global's trade-data product, publishes per-case research notes that track shipments around AD/CVD milestones:
  - Cold-rolled steel, May 18, 2016: imports were already falling when Commerce confirmed margins. https://panjiva.com/research/u-s-commerce-affirms-steel-dumping-but-imports-already-falling/1540
  - Large diameter pipe, Mar 5, 2018: possible acceleration of imports ahead of duties. https://es.panjiva.com/research/imports-flood-the-pipeline-as-antidumping-meets-national-security-duties/16067
  - The public excerpts do not name the underlying dataset. Panjiva's product is based on customs shipment records.
- Trade press uses import statistics to show country shifts after AD orders, for example the Home News Now mattress article (Aug 16, 2022) in C2.
- Purchaser search behavior in ITC data (nails, Pub 5370 p. II-23): purchasers typically contact 2 to 5 suppliers, and 14 of 44 widened or changed that number since 2019. This shows active market scanning, but it does not show which data they use.
- Gap: I found no public source showing an importer's sourcing team using Census or DataWeb country-by-product data to decide whether to qualify an alternative supplier. The evidence shows analysts, consultancies, trade press and the agency using it.

## Section E: not found, or uncertain

No practitioner advisory found states a rule like "act at the preliminary" in so many words. The timing evidence above is behavioral, plus the official cash-deposit rules.
- The AmCham press release (bot-check page), the Gartner primary release (403) and the SEIA Apr 26, 2022 analysis (403) were not read at the source. Figures from them are taken from press coverage, as noted.
- No SEC filing found states, for an AD/CVD case specifically, how many months it took to qualify a new supplier. Time-to-qualify numbers come from ITC purchaser questionnaires and from one trade-association submission (12 to 18 months) in USITC Pub 5405.
- ITC purchaser counts are self-reported and cover the whole period of investigation. In the negative cases (aluminum extrusions, tin mill, nails, PET), most reported switching was driven by supply shortages, COVID or bankruptcy rather than the duty. Purchasers who explicitly tie a switch to the trade case are a handful per report (PET: 2; forged steel fittings: several importers and purchasers, count not given; wine bottles: some, count not given). This weakens any claim that duty timing is the main driver of switching.
